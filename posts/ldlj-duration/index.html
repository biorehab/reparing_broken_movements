LDLJ: Effect of Threshold-Based Duration Estimation

Companion simulation for the blog post on the LDLJ measure of movement smoothness

In practice the true movement duration $T$ is rarely known exactly — it must be estimated from the recorded speed signal by detecting when the speed first exceeds, and last exceeds, some fraction of the peak speed. This page shows how sensitive LDLJ is to that choice.

A random movement composed of up to 8 minimum-jerk (MJT) submovements is generated on demand. The true onset and offset come directly from the simulation parameters. The estimated onset and offset are found by scanning the speed signal for the first and last sample that exceeds the chosen amplitude threshold. The duration slider rescales the entire movement in time (25%–200% of its original duration).

For LDLJ, because $T$ enters as $T^3$ in the normalisation, even a modest error in duration produces a substantial shift. SPARC, by contrast, cancels duration internally through a double normalisation — it is sensitive instead to which signal window enters the spectrum. This page lets you compare both effects side by side.

What to observe

A high threshold causes the estimated onset to be detected later and the estimated offset earlier than the true values, shrinking the estimated duration. Because LDLJ scales with $T^3$, even a 10% underestimate in $T$ shrinks the normalisation factor by ~27%, shifting the LDLJ value significantly.

SPARC behaves very differently. Unlike LDLJ, SPARC does not use duration as an explicit normalisation factor — its internal velocity normalisation by $v_p \cdot T$ is cancelled by a second normalisation by the DC component of the spectrum, so duration drops out entirely. SPARC is therefore insensitive to duration estimation errors per se. What it is sensitive to is which samples go into the Fourier transform: here we compute SPARC from the sliced window, so a different threshold means different signal content enters the spectrum. A narrower window excludes the slow onset/offset edges of the movement, altering the spectral shape and arc length.

Compare the % errors for LDLJ and SPARC — they reflect fundamentally different mechanisms: LDLJ error is driven by the $T^3$ factor (pure duration bias), while SPARC error comes from changes in spectral content at the movement boundaries.

A very low threshold (near 1%) brings the estimated window close to the true one. But in noisy recordings a low threshold becomes unreliable — spurious noise spikes will cause the onset to be detected much too early and the offset too late, including irrelevant signal at both ends.
