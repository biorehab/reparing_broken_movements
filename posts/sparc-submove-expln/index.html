Spectral Interference due to the Addition of Two Submovements

Companion simulation for the blog post on the SPARC measure of movement smoothness

The Fourier transform is a linear transformation. The sum of two Gaussian submovements results in a combined Fourier spectrum that is the sum of the Fourier spectra of the individual submovements.

Gaussian submovements have Gaussian Fourier magnitude spectra. Let $v_1(t)$ be a Gaussian submovement with peak time $\mu$ and width $\sigma$: $$v_1(t) = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(t - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$ Its Fourier transform is also Gaussian: $$\hat{V}_1(f) = \sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\,\exp\!\left(-2\pi^2 \sigma^2 f^2\right) e^{-i 2\pi f \mu}$$ When a second submovement $v_2(t) = a \cdot v_1(t - \Delta)$ is added, the combined spectrum is: $$\hat{V}(f) = \hat{V}_1(f)\left(1 + a\,e^{-i 2\pi f \Delta}\right)$$ The factor $\left|1 + a\,e^{-i 2\pi f \Delta}\right|$ is the one that leads to constructive and destructive interference at different frequencies, which modulates the magnitude spectrum of the movement speed profile. There is destructive interference at frequencies where $e^{-i 2\pi f \Delta} = -1$ and constructive interference where $e^{-i 2\pi f \Delta} = +1$. Note that when $a = 1$, there can be perfect destructive interference resulting in zero magnitude; these are dips in the Fourier magnitude spectrum that go to zero. The phasor diagram at a given frequency $f$ shows the complex Fourier coefficients of the two submovements as vectors in the complex plane; their vector sum gives the combined Fourier coefficient, and its length equals the value of the magnitude spectrum at that frequency.

Purpose of this simulation

This simulation demonstrates how constructive and destructive interference in the frequency domain, visualised using phasor diagrams, results in a complex Fourier magnitude spectrum when two submovements are added.

How to use it

Drag the two sliders. The interval slider moves the second submovement later in time. The amplitude slider controls how large it is relative to the first. Click anywhere on the spectrum plot to select a frequency; the solid vertical line marks the selected frequency and the phasor diagram updates accordingly. The dashed line tracks the current mouse position over the spectrum.

Explanation of the output

Because the Fourier transform is linear, the spectrum of the combined movement is the vector sum of the spectra of the two individual submovements at every frequency. The magnitude of this vector sum — visible in the spectrum plot — varies depending on the relative phase of the two phasors at that frequency.

At frequencies where the phasors are 180° out of phase, they cancel and the spectrum dips. At frequencies where they are in phase, they reinforce and the spectrum peaks. When the two submovements have equal amplitude ($a = 1$), the cancellation can be perfect, driving the spectrum to zero at those frequencies.
