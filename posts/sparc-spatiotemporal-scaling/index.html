SPARC: Spatiotemporal Scaling Invariance

Companion simulation for the article on the SPARC measure of movement smoothness

A useful smoothness measure should remain unchanged when the same movement is performed faster or slower, or with a larger or smaller range of motion — as long as its overall shape stays the same. SPARC satisfies this property: it is invariant under both amplitude scaling and temporal scaling.

Use this simulation to verify that claim. Generate a random movement, then adjust the amplitude scale $\alpha$ and the duration scale $\beta$. The baseline and scaled SPARC values are shown side by side — they should remain equal regardless of how you move the sliders.

Amplitude scaling multiplies the speed profile by $\alpha$, making the movement cover $\alpha$ times the original distance in the same amount of time. Temporal scaling stretches the movement duration by $\beta$ while dividing the speed by $\beta$, so the total displacement is preserved: $\int \alpha\,v(t/\beta)/\beta\;dt = \alpha\int v(u)\,du$.

Why is SPARC invariant?

Amplitude scaling. Multiplying every sample of $v(t)$ by $\alpha$ scales the entire spectrum by the same factor. Because SPARC normalises the spectrum so that its DC value equals one, $\alpha$ cancels exactly and the normalised spectrum — and therefore the arc length — is unchanged.

Temporal scaling. Stretching the movement by $\beta$ (and dividing speed by $\beta$ to preserve displacement) compresses the frequency axis by $1/\beta$: every feature that appeared at frequency $f$ now appears at $f/\beta$. At the same time, the upper limit of integration $f_c$ is compressed by the same factor. The integrand's shape is identical, just evaluated over a correspondingly compressed interval — so the arc length is unchanged.

In combination. The two effects are independent, so applying both simultaneously leaves SPARC unchanged. This is why SPARC remains a reliable smoothness measure regardless of the speed or range of motion with which the same movement is performed.
